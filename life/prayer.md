# The Lord's Prayer

Jesus taught his disciples to pray to the Father, seek his kingdom, depend on him for daily needs, forgive others, and ask for protection (Matthew 6:9–13). Matthew places this teaching in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5:1–2; 6:5–13). Luke records a disciple asking Jesus to teach them to pray after Jesus had been praying elsewhere (Luke 11:1–4). These are distinct narrative settings; we need not assume that Luke's question occurred during Matthew's sermon. Matthew's fuller wording provides the outline below, while Luke confirms the central themes.

Before giving the prayer, Jesus warns against praying to impress people or relying on many empty words (Matthew 6:5–8):

> But when you pray, go into your room and shut the door and **pray to your Father** who is in secret. And your Father who sees in secret will reward you. — Matthew 6:6 (ESV)

His warning concerns motives, not a ban on all prayer with others; the prayer itself says **“Our”** Father (Matthew 6:9). Jesus also says that the Father knows our needs before we ask (Matthew 6:8).

> Pray then like this:
>
> **Our Father in heaven**, **hallowed be your name**.  
> **Your kingdom come**, **your will be done**, on earth as it is in heaven.  
> **Give us this day our daily bread**,  
> and **forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors**.  
> And **lead us not into temptation**, but **deliver us from evil**.
>
> — Matthew 6:9–13 (ESV)

## The prayer's petitions

### Our Father in heaven

Jesus directs the prayer to **the Father** (Matthew 6:9), as he does when teaching the disciples to ask the Father in his name (John 16:23). Luke's account also opens with “**Father**” (Luke 11:2). The Father is not remote: he knows what his children need (Matthew 6:8, 32). This gives reason to approach him honestly rather than perform for an audience.

### Hallowed be your name

To hallow the Father's [name](../../word/terms/name.md) is to treat him as holy, not merely to say a formula. Isaiah speaks of people sanctifying God's name (Isaiah 29:23); God's promise also connects the holiness of his name with making himself known among the nations (Ezekiel 36:22–23). The prayer begins with the Father's honor before turning to our needs.

### Your kingdom come; your will be done

The petition asks for the Father's rule and purposes to take effect on earth (Matthew 6:10). It also calls us to seek his kingdom rather than make our own concerns ultimate (Matthew 6:33). Jesus himself prayed, “not as I will, but as **you will**” (Matthew 26:39, ESV). Prayer in his [name](../../word/terms/name.md) is not a way to force the Father to approve selfish plans (James 4:3; 1 John 5:14).

### Give us this day our daily bread

The request for bread names ordinary, recurring needs (Matthew 6:11). Israel's daily manna offers an earlier picture of dependence on the Father's provision (Exodus 16:4–5, 16–21). Later in the same sermon, Jesus says the Father knows our need for food and clothing and urges us not to be anxious about tomorrow (Matthew 6:25–34). That passage reinforces trust in provision; it does not claim to explain every line of the prayer.

### Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors

Jesus explicitly returns to this petition immediately after the prayer: if we forgive others, our heavenly Father will forgive us; if we refuse, he will not forgive our trespasses (Matthew 6:14–15). Forgiving others does not mean denying harm. It does mean that seeking the Father's mercy cannot be separated from [repentance](repentance.md) and a willingness to release others' debts (compare Psalm 32:5; Matthew 18:21–35).

### Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil

We ask the Father to guard us in trials and rescue us from evil (Matthew 6:13). Scripture does not describe God as enticing people to sin: “God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself **tempts no one**” (James 1:13, ESV). Jesus later told his disciples, “**Watch and pray** that you may not enter into temptation” (Matthew 26:41, ESV). Matthew 7:6, on pearls and pigs, addresses another matter; it is not an explanation of this petition. The phrase “from evil” can also be rendered “from the evil one,” so the request need not be limited to a single kind of danger.

## The doxology and the ending

Some translations include “For Yours is the **kingdom** and the **power** and the **glory** forever. **Amen**” at Matthew 6:13 (NKJV); the ESV omits this traditional doxology from its main text and notes it as a manuscript addition in a footnote. Luke's version does not contain it (Luke 11:2–4). These differences do not establish when or why the doxology entered Matthew's text. In the ESV, Matthew 6:13 is followed by teaching about forgiveness (Matthew 6:14–15).

The textual question does not establish that prayers must end with “Amen,” nor that saying “Amen” somehow ends the Father's attention. Paul's instruction to “**pray without ceasing**” (1 Thessalonians 5:17, ESV) calls for a continuing practice of prayer, not one uninterrupted spoken prayer.

## Jesus' example and his name

Jesus' own prayers illustrate his trust in the Father. Before raising Lazarus, he thanked the Father and explained that he spoke so the crowd might believe the Father sent him (John 11:41–42). In Gethsemane, he submitted his will to the Father's (Matthew 26:39, 42). He prayed for the disciples' protection in John 17:11–15; these examples reinforce the prayer's themes without implying that Matthew 6–7 is a verse-by-verse commentary on it.

Jesus tells his disciples to ask the Father in his [name](../../word/terms/name.md) (John 16:23–24). He also connects asking in his name with the Father's [glory](../../word/terms/glory.md) (John 14:13). To pray in Jesus' name is more than attaching a phrase to a request: the request should agree with the Father's will (1 John 5:14), and thanksgiving belongs in prayer (Philippians 4:6–7).

## Prayer with others

Paul urges prayers and thanksgivings for **all people**, including rulers (1 Timothy 2:1–4). He also asks men to pray in every place, “lifting **holy hands** without anger or quarreling” (1 Timothy 2:8, ESV). The emphasis includes reconciliation and a holy manner of life; the text does not forbid folded hands or require one posture at every prayer. Ezra knelt and spread out his hands when praying (Ezra 9:5). Jesus' warning against showy prayer concerns the desire to be seen, not the mere fact that others are present (Matthew 6:5–6).

## Who receives prayer?

### The Father and direct addresses to Jesus

Jesus' model prayer addresses the Father (Matthew 6:9), and his instruction about asking in his name identifies the Father as the one asked (John 16:23). Yet a blanket claim that believers never address Jesus directly would go beyond the evidence: Stephen says, “**Lord Jesus, receive my spirit**” (Acts 7:59, ESV). The ESV also reads “If you **ask me** anything in my name, I will do it” (John 14:14); some manuscripts omit “me.” The reading and the significance of direct addresses are discussed further in this [article on prayer to Jesus](https://son.ofgod.info/shema/prayer-to-jesus). Neither passage changes the addressee of the Lord's Prayer: the Father.

### The Holy Spirit, angels, and the dead

Jesus teaches disciples to ask the Father for good gifts; Luke explicitly mentions the Holy Spirit among those gifts (Matthew 7:7–11; Luke 11:13). These passages do not instruct disciples to direct prayer to the Holy Spirit. The [Spirit of God](../../ofgod/spirit/the-spirit-of-god.draft.md) is not presented as a competing recipient of the model prayer.

Paul warns against **worship of angels** (Colossians 2:18–19), but that warning alone does not equate every mention of angels with prayer to them. His teaching identifies “**one God**” and “**one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus**” (1 Timothy 2:5, ESV). Scripture also directs people away from consulting the dead and toward their God (Isaiah 8:19; compare Leviticus 19:31). These texts give no reason to replace the Father as the addressee Jesus taught.
